Schedule Management
Project schedule management is what most people think of when they think of project managers.

## PM06:4

What is project schedule?
• A project schedule is a model that presents linked activities with planned dates.
• Shows durations, milestones and the necessary people and resources required.
• When things start and end, and how activities roll up to milestones or releases.
• Once created, they can also be used to track actual project performance against and keep stakeholders informed of progress.

Define Activities

## PM06:11

Define Activities
• What: process of identifying and documenting the specific actions to be performed to produce the project deliverables.
• Why: decomposes work packages into schedule activities. • When: performed once or at predefined points in the project. • How:
• Start with the Work Breakdown Structure (WBS), which breaks the project scope into manageable work packages.
• Decompose these work packages into smaller, actionable activities that can be assigned, scheduled, and tracked.
• Example: For a work package like "Develop Website," activities might include "Design Homepage," "Code Backend," and "Test Functionality."

## PM06:23

Tools and Techniques
Rolling Wave planning
• An iterative planning technique in which the work to be accomplished in the near term is planned in detail, and the work further out is planned at a higher level. • Allows flexibility in dynamic or uncertain projects • Focuses effort where it's needed most (on upcoming work) • Adapts as more information becomes available

## PM06:24

RWP & Milestone list
• Milestones Anchor the Rolling Plan
• In RWP, detailed plans are created only for near-term work, while future work is outlined at a higher level.
• Milestones serve as anchors or checkpoints in the high-level schedule.
• Milestones Trigger Planning Waves
• As the project progresses and reaches a milestone, a new “wave” of planning is triggered.
• Each wave increases the detail level for the next segment of work up to the next milestone.
• RWP Helps Define Milestones Realistically
• Since RWP adapts as new information arises, it ensures that milestones are based on realistic scope and timelines, not just assumptions made at project initiation.

Milestones give clarity, accountability, and

and adaptability. Used together, they allow you to

Estimate Activity Durations

## PM06:38

Estimate Activity Durations
• What: process of estimating the number of work periods needed to complete individual activities with estimated resources. • Project resources: can be material, people, equipment, or anything else capable of definition (usually other than labour) required for the completion of a project activity.
• Why: provides the amount of time each activity will take to complete.
• When: performed throughout the project.
• How: • Consider the given assumptions and constraints (quality and quantity of resources, motivation of staff, deadline, ...) • Estimate activity duration with appropriate techniques (base on number of resources, the skill proficiency of them,…) • Determine the uncertainty and estimate contigent reserve. • Re-evaluate and determine the optimal way to complete the activity if needed • Finalize and document the duration estimates

## PM06:41

Affect factors to duration estimation
• Law of diminishing returns: After a certain point, adding more resources to a task will result in smaller and smaller gains in productivity—and may even make things worse
• Advances in technology: advances in technology may impact duration and resource needs
• Motivation of staff: • Student Syndrome: Waiting until the latest possible time to start (procrastination) • Parkinson’s law: Work expands to fill available time

## PM06:42

Tools & Techniques
Analogous Estimating
• Uses information from a previous, similar project, such as duration, budget, size and complexity for future project.
• Analogous estimate is generally less costly and time consuming but generally less accurate.
• This estimate will be more accurate if previous project is similar in nature and not just in appearance.
• Analogous duration estimating is frequently used to estimate project duration when there is a limited amount of detailed information about the project.

## PM06:43

Tools & Techniques
Parametric Estimating
• Parametric estimate uses a statistical relationship between historical data and other variables. • Identify a measurable parameter (e.g., cost per unit, hours per line of code, dollars per square meter). • Use a formula or rate based on historical data or benchmarks. • Multiply the parameter by the quantity needed for the current project.
• Example - A resource will take 20hrs per module and hence 50 modules will take 1000hrs (50X20 = 1000hrs)

## PM06:44

Tools & Techniques
Three point estimates (PERT)
• When there is insufficient historical data or when using judgmental data.
• A three-point estimate uses average of optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic estimates and hence improving the accuracy. • Pessimistic (tP) assumes the worst case scenario • Most likely (tM) – The realistic and most likely estimate • Optimistic (tO) is the best case scenario.
• tE = (tO + tM + tP)/3

• Contingency Reserve time —also called time reserves

Develop Schedule

## PM06:52

Develop Schedule
• What: process of analyzing activity sequences, durations, resource requirements, and schedule constraints to create a schedule model for project execution and monitoring and controlling. It generates a schedule model with planned dates for completing project activities.
• Why: serve as a baseline to track progress • When: performed throughout the project (iterative process).

## PM06:53

Develop Schedule

How?
• Once the project start or finish dates have been imposed , it is common to have the project staff assigned to the activities review their assigned activities. The project schedule is then analyzed to:
• Estimate the minimum project duration (critical path) and determine the amount of schedule flexibility.

• Determine conflicts with resource calendar and if resource optimization is required
• Determine conflict with milestones and if schedule compression is required
• Review and revise the duration estimates, resource estimates, and schedule reserves needed for risks
• Establish an approved project schedule that can serve as a baseline to track progress.

Tools & Techniques
Critical Path Method (CPM)
• CPM is used to calculate the critical path(s) and the amount of schedule flexibility (total float).
• Often activities happen in parallel, so the overall project duration is not the sum of all the activity estimates.
• The critical path is the longest path (by activity durations) through the project network diagram, also represents the shortest time the project can be finished in (any delays to activities on this path will delay the Finish date).

## PM06:57

Critical Path Method
Schedule flexibility (float) determination
• Free Float: The amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the early start of any immediate successor.
• Total Float: The maximum amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project’s finish date.
• Activity on the critical path -> float of zero.
Total Float = Late Start (LS) – Early Start (ES) = Late Finish (LF) – Early Finish (EF)

## PM06:59

Early/late start & early/late finish

Network Diagram Nodes
• ES = Earliest date an activity can be started
• EF = Earliest date an activity can be completed
• LS = Latest date an activity can start • LF = Latest date an activity can be
completed

Early Start & Early Finish Calculation
• Forward Pass: to determine the earliest start and finish date
• Early Finish (EF) = Early Start (ES) + Duration - 1
Late Start & Late Finish Calculation
• Backward Pass: to determine the latest start and finish date
• Late Start (LS) = Late Finish (LF) – Duration + 1

## PM06:61

Critical float notice
• Total Float on this CP may be positive or negative (zero is the normal and expected condition)
• Positive: The project has built-in buffer time or slack in the critical path (unusual and rare).
• Negative: the project is already behind schedule based on constraints or deadlines.
• The project is in schedule risk or noncompliant with deadlines. • Requires schedule compression (e.g., crashing or fast tracking) or revisiting
constraints.
• A project can have multiple critical paths • Higher risk: If any critical path is delayed, the whole project is delayed. • Makes project control and recovery planning more complex.
• A near-critical path is a sequence of activities that is almost as long as the critical path — usually has low total float (e.g., 1–2 days).
• Not currently critical, but can become critical with minor delays. • High-risk areas for float erosion.

Resource Smoothing
• Technique to adjust activities to meet predefined resources limits

• Project’s critical path is not changed and completion date may not be delayed

Tools & Techniques: Data Analysis

What-If Scenario Analysis
• A predictive technique used to evaluate the impact of specific hypothetical changes on the project schedule.
• What if the situation represented by scenario “X” happens?
• Used to accesses the feasibility of the project schedule under adverse conditions

Simulation
• Simulation models the combined effects of individual project risks and other sources of uncertainty to evaluate their potential impact on achieving project objectives.
• Monte Carlo Simulation: a quantitative risk analysis technique that uses probability distributions and simulations to analyze uncertainty in the schedule.

Tools & Techniques: Leads and Lags
Leads and Lags
• Refinements applied during network analysis to develop a viable schedule by adjusting the start time of the successor activities
• Leads are used to advance a successor activity with respect to the predecessor activity
• Lags are used where processes require a set period of time to elapse between the predecessors and successors without work or resource impact.

## PM06:68

Tools & Techniques: Schedule Compression
Shorten or accelerate the schedule duration without reducing the project scope in order to meet schedule constraints, imposed dates, or other schedule objectives. • Crashing: adding resources (ex.
approving overtime, bringing in additional resources, or paying to expedite delivery to activities on the critical path). => increased cost • Fast Tracking: Critical activities that would normally be done in sequence are allowed to be done in parallel or with some overlap => may result in rework and increases the risk. (Communication requirements increases during fast tracking).
