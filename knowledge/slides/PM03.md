## PM03:9

Feasibility Analysis
Feasibility analysis is the process of evaluating whether a proposed project is viable, realistic, and worth pursuing. It helps organizations decide whether to proceed with a project idea before investing significant time and resources. Information may includes: • Technically feasibility • Economic feasibility • Operational Feasibility • Legal Feasibility • Schedule Feasibility

## PM03:10

Murder board
A Murder Board is a critical review panel used in project selection (especially in high-stakes environments like government, defense, or corporate R&D) to rigorously evaluate, challenge, and potentially “kill” weak or unviable projects before they get approved. Steps: 1. Project team presents their proposal. 2. A group of experts, skeptics, or senior
reviewers (the “murder board”) acts as critics. 3. They ask tough questions, challenge assumptions, and test feasibility. 4. Based on the outcome: The project may be approved, revised, or rejected (“murdered”).

## PM03:11

Scoring models
A Scoring Model is a structured, quantitative method used to evaluate and compare multiple project proposals based on predefined criteria. Steps: 1. Define evaluation criteria 2. Assign weights to each
criterion 3. Score each project against
every criterion 4. Calculate weighted scores 5. Compare total scores

## PM03:15

Develop Project charter
What?
The document that: • formally authorizes the existence of a project • provides the project manager with the authority
to apply organizational resources to project activities
When?
Take place on a project/or phase before it becomes official
Who?
Issued by the project initiator or sponsor (project manager may help)

## PM03:16

How?
• Validate project alignment with organizational strategy and expected business value.
• Identify key deliverables, initial milestones, based on the business requirements
• Identify high level risks, assumptions, and constraints • Evaluation of the feasibility of new products or services within the given
assumptions and/or constraints. • Propose an implementation strategy • Obtain project charter approval from the sponsor • Inform stakeholders of the approved project charter

## PM03:17

Define Project objectives
• Project success should be measured with consideration toward achievement of the project objectives.
• Project objectives should be documented and agreed upon by the key stakeholders and the project manager.
• Project managers needs to be able to: • Clearly document the project objectives (S.M.A.R.T objectives) • Resolve competing objectives, balance the demands • Prioritize project objectives

## PM03:28

Outputs: Assumption logs
• High-level strategic and operational assumptions and constraints are normally identified in the business case before the project is initiated and will flow into the project charter.
• Lower-level activity and task assumptions are generated throughout the project such as defining technical specifications, estimates, the schedule, risks, etc.
• The assumption log is used to record all assumptions and constraints throughout the project life cycle.

## PM03:29

Create Assumption Logs
• What are assumptions?
• Things believed to be true during planning
• Who provides them?
• PM, sponsor, stakeholders, technical experts
• When are they created?
• Mainly in Initiating and Planning phases
• Why are they important?
• For planning accuracy, risk identification, expectation alignment

## PM03:36

Identify Stakeholders
• What:
• Identifying project stakeholders regularly • analyzing and documenting relevant information regarding their
interests, involvement, interdependencies, influence, and potential impact on project success.
• Why:
• An un-identified key stakeholder can potentially turn high risk to project
• Identify the appropriate focus for engagement of each stakeholder or group of stakeholders.
• When:
• As early as possible, preferably during initiation and definitely prior to starting of planning.
• Periodically throughout the project as needed.

## PM03:42

Tools & Techniques: Data representation

Grid models
• Power/Interest grid, Power/Influence grid, Impact/influence grid
• useful for small projects or for projects with simple relationships between stakeholders and the project, or within the stakeholder community itself

• Power: level of authority • Interest: level of concern
about the project’s outcomes • Influence: ability to influence the outcomes of the project • Impact: ability to cause changes to the project’s planning or execution

## PM03:43

Tools & Techniques: Data representation
Stakeholder Cube
a refinement of the grid models • Power: Refers to the degree of
influence a stakeholder has on the project's outcomes. • Interest: Indicates the level of involvement or concern a stakeholder has in the project. • Attitude: Describes the stakeholder's support or opposition to the project.

## PM03:44

Tools & Techniques: Data representation
Salience Model
Describes classes of stakeholders based on assessments of their power, urgency (need for immediate attention or action, either time-constrained or relating to the stakeholders’ high stake in the outcome), and legitimacy (their involvement is appropriate) • useful for large complex
communities of stakeholders or where there are complex networks of relationships within the community. • also useful in determining the relative importance of the identified stakeholders.

Tools & Techniques: Data representation
Directions of influence
Classifies stakeholders according to their influence on the work of the project or the project team itself • Upward: senior management,
sponsor, steering committee • Downward: team members or
experts • Outward: outside the project
team( suppliers, government departments, the public, endusers, and regulators) • Sideward: the peers of the project manager such as other project managers or middle managers
